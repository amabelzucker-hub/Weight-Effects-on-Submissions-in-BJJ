Moving up a weight class

Does changing weight class change how often BJJ competitors finish opponents, or get finished themselves? A rebuild of last spring's project with cleaned weight data, a Callaway & Sant'Anna estimator, and competitors who moved up compared against those who moved down. Finishing opponents rises after any change of class, and rises more for movers down, mostly through chokes. Getting finished shows no clear direction effect, except that getting caught in leg locks rises after a change of class in either direction.

What the data say

Every match is from one competitor's point of view, so a submission ending is either a win by submission (they finished the opponent) or a loss by submission (they got finished). Before moving, about of a competitor's decided weight-class matches are submission wins and are submission losses. These are BJJ Heroes profiles of notable competitors, so wins outnumber losses.

Each effect below is split into two parts. The direction effect is half the gap between movers up and movers down: the part that depends on which way the competitor moved. The common shift is the average of the two: the part that comes with changing class at all. Both groups are compared with the same competitors who haven't moved, using one shared bootstrap.

Finishing opponents. Movers down finish 11.0 points more often after the move, movers up 2.7. That's a common shift of and a direction effect of , consistent with being the relatively bigger competitor making finishes easier. The gap is mostly chokes: movers down finish 7.9 points more by choke, a direction effect of . One caution: even counting only matches at their old weight, movers down were already finishing more in the year before they moved (about +16 points, based on 11 competitors).

Getting finished. No clear effect either way. Movers up get finished 1.4 points more, movers down 2.3 points less: a direction effect of , in the direction you'd expect but not distinguishable from zero. The exception is leg locks. Both groups get caught in leg locks more after changing class, a common shift of on a base of about 2%. Finishing with leg locks rises too, though less clearly. Together these point to competitors entering more leg-lock-friendly events (no-gi or submission-only) around a change of class.

Placebo checks

Placebo checks ask whether movers were already drifting away from non-movers before they moved. The first version failed too many: 16 of 70 points excluded zero, where chance would give about 3–4. Most movers had already fought some matches at their new weight in the years before the move, which leaked after-move behaviour into the "before" side. Four changes, applied to the placebo checks:

  • Only matches at the usual weight. Placebo outcomes count only matches within 2kg of a competitor's typical weight (their before-move median for movers). A year counts if at least 3 such matches remain. The same filter applies to the comparison competitors. This keeps 236 of 260 before-move years and two-thirds of their matches.
  • Compared with the last clean year. Each before-move year is compared with the competitor's last usable before-move year, so the years don't need to be consecutive.
  • Match-count weighting. A change between two 20-match seasons counts far more than one between two 5-match seasons. This applies to the after-move estimates too.
  • At least 4 movers per point. Points resting on fewer are dropped.

Movers down can now be placebo-tested (15 competitors). For getting finished, the checks fail about as often as chance would predict. For finishing opponents they fail about twice as often, mostly because of the movers-down rise in the year before the move.

Expanding the sample

The first version used only competitors who changed class exactly once, compared against competitors who never moved. Two changes add data:

  • First moves of multi-movers. A competitor who changed class more than once counts for their first move, if the new class held into the next year. Their later years are dropped. This adds 33 movers up and 23 movers down. Another 135 competitors still don't qualify, mostly because of one-season trips into another division.
  • Not-yet-movers as controls. Competitors who move later also serve as comparisons before their own move. This barely changes anything, because the 233 never-movers dominate the comparison group.

What was broken, and the fix

1

Federation labels looked like weight changes

Weight labels come from different rulebooks: AJP −85kg, IBJJF −82.3/−88.3kg, ADCC −88kg, AJP −56kg vs IBJJF −57.5kg. Under a fixed label-to-class mapping, many competitors seemed to move up just by entering an IBJJF event after an AJP one. The fix: use each competitor's median labelled weight per year, and count a move only when it shifts by at least 5kg (one real class step) and stays there. Wobble of up to 4kg counts as the same class.

2

"Changed exactly once" failed on noise

Under the strict rule, half of all competitors bounced between classes, often because of a single match in one year. Years with fewer than 3 weight-class matches are now dropped (2.5% of matches), and open-class (absolute) matches are excluded, since they have no weight class. Competitors who moved more than once count for their first move.

3

Two-way fixed effects mixes up the comparisons

A standard fighter-plus-year fixed-effects regression with staggered timing uses already-moved competitors as controls for later movers. That can bias the estimate. The rebuild estimates each cohort-by-year effect separately against competitors who haven't moved yet, then averages them weighted by cohort size.

4

Gaps in competitors' careers

Competitors skip years, so the panel has holes. Each comparison uses only competitors observed in both of its years. Individual comparisons were recalculated by hand from the panel; those checks now run as automated tests, and the estimator reproduces the R did package exactly on a balanced panel.

5

After-move matches leaked into the placebo checks

Movers often fight some matches at their new weight before their typical weight changes. Placebo checks now count only matches at the usual weight. See the placebo section above.

Robustness

The common shift in finishing opponents is positive at every threshold and clear in 4 of 5. The direction effect on finishing is negative everywhere but at least two standard errors from zero in only 3 of 5, two of them just barely. The choke-win direction effect behaves the same way. The leg-lock-losses common shift is clear in 4 of 5, all but the 2-match setting. The direction effect on getting finished is never clear.

Limits to keep in mind

  • The data are sparse. With 86 movers up and 51 down, only effects of several points can be detected for finishing. Submission losses are rare (under 8% of matches, under 2% for any single submission), so loss estimates for specific submissions are very imprecise.
  • Changing class isn't random. Competitors change class as they mature, gain or cut weight, or chase specific brackets. Comparing changes rather than levels removes fixed differences between competitors, but not trends that line up with the move.
  • Movers down were on an upswing just before moving. At their old weight, their finishing rate rose about 16 points in the year before the move (11 competitors), mostly in chokes. If that rise would have continued anyway, the finishing gap overstates the direction effect. If it was a hot season settling back, the gap understates it. The data can't tell which.
  • The up/down split assumes the same career effect for both groups. Movers down started heavier and may be cutting for specific events. If their career shift differs from the movers-up group's, part of it ends up in the direction effect.
  • The after-move estimates use the year before the move as their starting point. That year can include some matches at the new weight. Only the placebo checks filter them out.
  • Gi and no-gi are mixed. The data don't record gi vs. no-gi, and leg-lock rules differ by ruleset. The leg-lock results may reflect a change in events entered rather than anything about the new division.
  • Most moves are recent. 30 of 86 movers up moved in 2021–23, and 2023 holds only early-season events.
  • The data are BJJ Heroes profiles. They cover notable competitors and record their own matches, so wins outnumber losses and lesser-known competitors are underrepresented.

Code and reproducibility

Everything on this page is produced by the code in this project's repository. Run python3 bjj_staggered_did.py data.csv results for the main analysis and python3 -m pytest for the 60 tests. Every analysis choice is a named setting in bjj_did/config.py. The match data isn't redistributed; it comes from rdreese89/Web-Scraper, which scraped it from BJJ Heroes.
